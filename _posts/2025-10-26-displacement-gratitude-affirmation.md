### Foreword

I wrote this essay to explain myself. The last eight years have had a profound impact on my worldview, and as I return home to begin a new chapter of life, I feel compelled to weave together the threads that connect my past to my present. 

I considered long and hard, before ultimately deciding to release my writing into the public domain. Part of me is rightly terrified at what revealing this much about myself can bring, not only from those who may oppose me in the future, but also from my own friends and family. The other part of me insists that _this needs to be said, nomatter what will come of it._ That if bringing beauty into the light exposes my own ugliness, then I must accept that as well.

I've collected the impressions, thought processes, and mindsets recorded here from my journal entries, text messages, and also, from my own recollections. I've done my best to represent myself truthfully, but constructing a narrative from memory always introduces subjectivity and the presence of hindsight, and so I acknowledge that there may be motivations and impulses unknown to, or hidden from, myself.

The title is a reference to Peter Singer's classic essay on ethics, **[Famine, Affluence, and Morality](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org/get-involved/videos-books-and-essays/famine-affluence-and-morality-peter-singer)**.

This essay contains spoilers to Fyodor Dostoevsky's **[The Brothers Karamazov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Brothers_Karamazov)** and Hayao Miyazaki's **[Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nausica%C3%A4_of_the_Valley_of_the_Wind_(manga))**. Earlier this year, I tried to write an academic essay about these two books, but I could not finish it without putting my heart into it. I then started to write an polemic essay to shame my peers into introspecting more seriously about their own lives and the impact of their unaccountable, irresponsible use of their privileges, but then I started introspecting into the contradictions of own life. I then started to write about myself, but I could not do it without these books.

So here we are.

## Part 1: Displacement towards Gratitude

### Rebellion

*"Look at yourself. You disgust me. You've been given enormous privilege and talent, and you are wasting it. You want to take on the burden of saving the world? You can't even be strong to yourself."*

I had a habit of winning, though I tried to be humble about it. When one is already successful and is already perceived as being such, one can afford an image of humility, knowing that the adulation of others will always be present to nourish one's arrogance. Graduating from high school, I had done well in many things, from music to martial arts, from scorecards to scholarships. Entering and excelling in my first year at the University of British Columbia, half a world away, I found little to shake the belief that I can achieve great things in life.

I remembered caring deeply about humanity, albeit in an abstract sense, for most of my early teenage years. In a personal way, I was appalled at the pitiful conditions that many Indonesians endure daily, and the abysmal state of our built and natural environment. More broadly, I was deeply affected by the events of my day: The Egyptian Revolution of 2011 and El-Sisi's crackdown, the Syrian Civil War and the migrant crisis that followed, the Lybian Revolution and the ensuing protracted conflict. And I studied the history of our world, from the ideologies and wars and genocides, to the ongoing climate crisis.

A recurring theme in my upbringing was me, quite bluntly, asking my family, teachers, and friends why they refuse to consider the possibility of a better world, and to work towards one. And having read the Holy Bible cover to cover and having become a devout Christian, it frustrated me to no end that the Church did not seem to believe in brighter and more compassionate futures either. All the worldly issues that they troubled themselves to lecture about concerned the treatment of homosexuals, the translation of the Bible for evangelization, the errors of other Christian traditions, the creationist narrative, and the nature of the salvation of the elect. There were charity drives and community activities to help the impoverished, of course, but never systematic nor sustained. Never something inalienable from salvation.

I think my moment of alienation came when I recognized hell on earth, while my church and my community remained in denial about it:

_"Look at your feet. Do you see the millions scrabbling desparately in the slums and refugee camps for another tomorrow - not for a better future, but merely for shattered, broken survival? Extend your gaze towards the horizon. Do you see the looming shadow of climate catastrophe? Consider your own children. Do you want to raise them in such a world? Raise a fist against heaven. "You disgust me. You've created the keys to paradise, but you lock its gates against us."_

_"And look at what you've been able to achieve, when you put your mind to it. If man is utterly free to realize his own plan for the world, but if he wins this freedom by rejecting the apathetic capriciousness of God, then he is constrained to conduct himself with altruistic, benevolent strength."_

So I resolved to be stronger than I had ever been before, for everyone I wanted to be strong for. I enrolled in my university's most challenging engineering program. I took up a lead organizing role at an engineering club. And I volunteered to lead a project for a design team. And so by learning to be strong, I would develop the capacity to do good.

But when the time came for me to be both, I could not bring myself to be either. I kept missing the chances handed to me. Every one of my successes became marred by another failure. I had too little time, energy, and willpower, to actualize the achievement I had envisioned for myself. If self-responsibility, not higher power, underpinned my entire value structure, then by failing to achieve my potential, I had failed myself on a fundamental level. If my sense of self was predicated upon the affirmation of my successes, a proxy for my ability to be responsible for myself, then I undermine myself by failing.

Looking back, I realize that I had made enormous progress during those years, both technically and interpersonally. But critically, the failure that I had ascribed to God had become my own: _"Look at yourself. You disgust me. You've been given enormous privilege and talent, and you are wasting it. You want to take on the burden of saving the world? You can't even be strong to yourself."_

### Despair

*"The world is coming to an end, but somehow I am still here. Why am I still here?"*

As my faith in my own abilities eroded, I began confronting my philosophy of self-responsibility. Throughout my childhood, I had taken responsibility over my work, and I had enjoyed the fruits of my successes. I had extended assistance to my peers openhandedly, believing that if they show responsibility over their academics, they have every right to succeed.

Yet, during those days, nomatter how tired I became, nomatter how much I made myself responsible for, I could not manifest decisive proof of my abilities. At the time, I saw three possible reasons for my predicament, that (1) I was not pushing myself hard enough, (2) I had grossly overestimated myself from the very beginning, or (3) My old mindset wasn't working and it needed to evolve.

A strong mindset requires internal consistency, consistency requires immutable axioms, and I believed that my determination came from my burning desire to mitigate climate change. And so began a multi-year project to burn away everything that was weak and inconsistent about my actions and personality. I became a vegetarian and a zero-waste enthusiast. I redoubled my commitments to my renewable energy engineering teams projects. I meditated on the roots of my shortcomings.

March 2020. My engineering project was dangerously overdue for a competition deadline. My academics were still in shambles and I was in danger of losing my scholarship. My summer internship was cancelled due to the pandemic. And like a violin with its soundpost knocked out, I still could not make a sound, could not resonate to save myself.

It was almost the end for me. And outside, the world was falling apart even faster. Thousands were dying, millions suffering. Economies in collapse, wildfires ravaging whole continents. And the hundred million refugees and IDPs, with nowhere to go and nowhere to hide from the pandemic. Death stalked the world and there was no hope.

However, by some miracle, I pulled through. I saved my grades and retained my scholarship. The engineering competition was cancelled, to both my disappointment and relief. A company even called back and offered to restore my internship.

I was fortunate, while the whole world suffered. Millions of people were enduring terrible hardship, with a resilience and responsibility I could only hope to muster. If there was any justice, they should have lived while I died.

Why am I alive when so many have died? Why am I allowed hope amidst such catastrophe?

I am not causally related to the suffering human across the waters; her misfortune could not have been my luck. And yet, now having lost a small part of myself, I begin to reach across to the damaged parts of others. Having died a little to myself, I begin to see others struggling with both living and dying. And having received hope when I least expected it, I begin to understand the resilient beauty of this world.

As I started receiving paychecks from my internship work, I was struck with a realization: That this was not my money.

### To find tomorrow

*"I cannot succeed - I've failed today and I will keep on failing. But if I continue onwards, I can still help somebody."*

I gave in small amounts at first. When something good happened to me, when I felt a sense of gratitude. I started with large organizations such as the UNHCR, UNICEF, and MSF, reasoning that they have strong operating records and the best chance of reaching the most marginalized people in the world.

Gratitude evokes a desire to be charitable, but it also opens a world of possibility. Being charitable exposed me to the struggles of other people. Understanding this struggle made me more acutely aware of the suffering that permeates existence. And out of this knowledge of suffering arises all at once, compassion, anger, desperation, calling me to be increasingly charitable, to alleviate suffering to the best of my ability.

What started as a momentary impulse became a fundamental aspect of my moral worldview.

But why act to alleviate suffering, when suffering returns and returns again, a mark of existence itself? Without God, there is no inherent purpose in suffering and no divine imperative to relieve suffering. There are still rational, humanitarian, and secular reasons to prevent suffering, but people's lives will always be punctuated by terrible agony and random, senseless devastation.

Is our presence here a mistake, then? Every new moment of existence lends itself to the displacement, neglect, and outright slaughter of innocent lives. Recalling _"Those who walk away from Omelas"_, one wonders if by virtue of being alive, one becomes responsible for the misery of a child across the world. If minimising suffering is the only goal, all that humanity needs to do is to... succumb.

Is all morality and virtue only a pale substitute to cessation? Are we all simply too ignorant to realize the virtues of non-existence? 

Yet even as I accepted the logic of non-being, stemming from a materialistic, dispassionate approach towards minimising suffering, I continued to yearn for the fire which had first opened my heart to suffering. 

When Greg Egan constructed living, conscious universes out of nothing but dust in his science fiction novel _'Permutation City'_, I lay awake pondering if the human spirit can really persist in, even arise out of, mere random numbers and their permutations. When Nausicaa from _'Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind'_ gave her own life to avert the destruction of her people, I remembered why I wanted to be strong in the first place.. When Hayao Miyazaki's _"Princess Mononoke"_ proclaimed _"Life is suffering. It is hard. The world is cursed. But still you find reasons to keep living"_, it dawned on me that there are forces stronger than reason, greater than suffering, that drive the human condition.

Cautiously, I began to entertain hope, hope as a gentle wind streaming through the iron latticework of rationality and utility. Idealistic hope that we can explore and understand the structure of our affliction, and that each of us can look past our bounded rationality and contribute to an inclusive solution. Desperate hope that the children in the refugee camps can hold on just a little longer, just until the war ends and they can return home. Ecological hope, that nature is stronger and more resilient than man's capacity for destruction.

And a quiet hope that I will be able to endure hardship without compromising myself. As Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind has done, that I can willingly do anything, give up everything, to protect the ones whom I love.

In those days of uncertainty, I was thankful to the winds of hope for guiding the way. In the midst of questioning my moral foundations, I had a nightmare where I died a violent death to save a bus full of passengers; I lay awake for a long time after that, wondering if I was capable, in real life, of such self-sacrifice. When I was ridden with self-doubt, I received a letter thanking me for my continued support of refugee rights and livelihoods. That night, I held that letter close, realizing that, in that moment, they helped me as much as I have helped them.

The process of creating a new framework for morality and action was a long and arduous one. But, it taught me that despite all my failings and inadequacies, I can still reach out and help someone. And that if I press onwards to tomorrow, perhaps someone else can find tomorrow as well.


## Part 2: Gratitude towards Affirmation

### The right to be happy

*Nausicaa: I feel so strange, as if the inside of my body has become transparent.*

*Selm: Its almost dawn.*

*Nausicaa: Its beautiful. I feel as if I've been reborn. I can't stop crying.*

*Selm: Are you cold?*

*Nausicaa: No. I was just wondering if its alright to be so happy.*

\- Selm and Nausicaa, in *Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind*

Radical hope therefore became a cornerstone of my sense of self. Without achievements to be proud of, without self-esteem, always seemingly cursed to fail, I pushed forward in the hope that I can still be someone to someone else. In the hope that I can attain myself and my responsibilities on this earth. In the hope that amidst the overwhelming despair of a climate catastrophe, I can one day **matter** in the great work of holding the world together.

I just needed one more chance to prove myself.

And I got it.

---

*Clarity. A door flung open, a bursting thundercloud, a roaring monsoon river. For years I have searched for a way that I could work on climate change mitigation, for a way to prevent so much suffering from being inflicted on the world. By improving the capabilities of renewable energy installations worldwide, my work will help clean energy achieve a new standard of quality. Because of what I will do, fewer people will starve and thirst, lose their homes, face scarcity-induced conflict…but only if I succeed.*

*Elation. A soul ascendant on a whirlwind. A pouring rain on a parched earth. A green leaf out from the winter. I have put so much of myself into this, into trying to help, but the problems just swallow them all. Giving to orphanages, but systematic poverty just makes more orphans. Donating to refugees, but the underlying conflict remains unresolved. Contributing to international medical aid, but food and water scarcity perpetuates the suffering of the ailing. But now, a chance to fight the underlying problems at the source, which will only get worse due to climate change.*

*Resolve. A fire in my heart. A driving wind at a sail, the crushing advance of a glacier. I cross the threshold, and I greet the vastness of the world. The road goes on, and on, stars alight on the cosmic firmament. There are things that I have not seen, things I do not know, but I will go and see this brave new world and know it well.*

*- Journal Entry Number 24, Vancouver, March 2021*

---

That night, I sat on the floor, folding my legs between my arms, shivering. I had just experienced something that was beyond me. It wasn't just the satisfaction of landing another internship, nor was it the contentment of being rewarded. To me, it was the affirmation of a fundamental principle that I could neither demarcate nor elucidate.

For a brief moment, my world was radiant. I had begun a career-changing internship; I had retained my scholarship again; After a winter of doubt, I had found a reason to be utterly and completely happy. Summer had come, literally and figuratively.

That week, a childhood friend died in her sleep. She was studying to be a doctor. She had been working around the clock to mitigate the pandemic. She was only 20 years old.

Her friends gathered to mourn, from every corner of the world. We sent for flowers, attended her funeral Mass, and raised a donation to Doctors without Borders in her name.

I held her in my thoughts, but I could not feel grief - only another emptiness. I contributed from my first paycheque in her memory, and reflected on the cruel irony it represented. I recalled her amicability, her spirit of volunteerism, her tenacity within an unforgiving academic system. But, as long as I had known her, I could only feel a stark, desolate, numbness.

I wondered if I had become inhuman. Had I lost the humanity to mourn, or had I already grieved for the entire world beforehand? 68 children were massacred in Kabul the same week - had my friend become just another statistic in my mind, or had I accepted the death of one friend, 69 times?

I attended the funeral mass, and though I had stopped believing, I could not help but yearn to see my friend again. I reflected upon the Christian conception on heaven and hell, and how it informs our attitudes towards death and salvation. 

Should someone die, and be brought to Saint Peter at the gate of heaven, how does one answer the challenge of "How can thou presume to enter into communion with God?"? By reciting the Nicene Creed or the Westminster Confession, demanding salvation through an orthodoxy of faith? By good works, though earthly benevolence cannot be extricated from exploitation? To bow ones head, admit one's guilt before God, and to walk away? No, one can only recognize one's sin and beg for forgiveness, without any presumption of deservedness.

And yet, should someone die, and upon entering full communion with God, be given the same choice presented to Jesus Christ in that extratemporal age: To descend into that place of eternal torment to take the place of one's tormentor in life, or to turn one's back on one's neighbour for an eternity of bliss alone?

Can we truly enjoy heaven in good conscience, knowing that its glory outshines, but never diminishes, the anguish of the damned? Can we truly fear hellfire, if a single incursion of self-sacrificial love closes all the distance between heaven and hell?

What is Christianity, beyond its promise of salvation after death? Could salvation be, as well, a material hope for the suffering and the living?

During the day, I threw myself into my work. I built frameworks and systems that would remain in use long after I had left. I worked long hours, driven by a newfound sense of concordance between myself and my place in this world. I continued to give much of my salary to humanitarian aid.

At night, I lay awake, troubled yet fascinated by the ideas and perspectives opened up by my stumbling forwards. Moral philosophy, the great belief systems, the everpresent anxiety of climate catastrophe, experience and reflection, permutations arising within permutations in an effort to arrive at a system to understand my world.

In those days, I sometimes felt that everything would be alright, that I could finally be content with myself and enjoy the summer warmth. But, those moments were fleeting. In everything I did, I felt like a nascent core of self-image drowning in an ocean of my inadequacies.

I felt as though lasting happiness was always ahead of me, always conditional, always something that I had no inherent right to.

### Famine, Affluence, and Morality

*"If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, then we ought, morally, to do it."*

\- Peter Singer, *Famine, Affluence, and Morality*

Entering my final year of university, I remained deeply troubled by what I had seen, and this had a major impact on my academic performance. Where I once had trouble waking up, intrusive thoughts now kept me awake all night. Beyond my work on the energy transition, my support for humanitarian aid, and my struggle to excel academically, I was locked in a desperate struggle for a sense of self.

*"Pass your courses, do not disappoint your family"*, I told myself every day. *"Graduate. When you start working and start earning a salary, then you can start helping people again."*

I graduated in 2022. I wasn't even that excited about receiving my degree - at that moment, all I wanted to do was to work on climate change, to contribute to humanitarian aid, and to figure out who I was. On a mountaintop in Bamff, Alberta, I swore not to let up, not to turn my back on the great challenges that had, and continue to, define me.

I found work in the renewable energy industry, and I started earning a decent salary. Living simply, eating vegetarian, and finding an affordably-priced rental meant that I started saving rapidly. My spreadsheets estimate that should these trends continue, I would be able to save a hundred thousand CAD within three years. That is more money than most Indonesians will ever see in a lifetime.

Millions of Ukrainians had just fled their homes due to war. Millions of Syrians are still refugees. Millions of Yemenis face famine and starvation. Millions of Rohingya had just experienced genocide. Sudan had erupted into civil war again. The world is burning. There will be 200 million climate refugees in the near future.

And even within affluent Vancouver, one feels the growing desperation. The rents that rise faster than the incomes, the overwhelmed food banks, the new persons lying slumped and senseless in front of the polished marble and bright glass.

As before, something deep within me told me that it was wrong to accumulate so much wealth while so much suffering persists. Whether it be the voice of reason, the logic of utility, the crying out of conscience, or the very Word of God, I could not ascertain.

I recalled the work of the utilitarians, from the calculus of Jeremy Bentham to the 'drowning child' thought experiments of Peter Singer, from Giving What We Can's effective altruism to my friends who stand by it. As Peter Singer writes in his seminal essay, "**Famine, Affluence, and Morality**", _"It makes no moral difference whether the person I can help is a neighbor's child ten yards away from me or a Bengali whose name I shall never know, ten thousand miles away. If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, then we ought, morally, to do it"._

I remembered my Christian upbringing, and the ideas about tithing and charity that emerged from it. I surfaced the bitterness and rage of my turning away from God, and the promise I made that I would be at least twice as compassionate, twice as generous and altruistic, as those self-satisfied Christians. I resumed my struggle to find faith again, apart from the self-righteous moralizing and threats of hellfire, but instead emerging from the transformation of the soul through suffering and self-sacrifice into salvation.

I channeled the pain, the quiet, childlike sobbing in a dark corner of my mind called 'conscience'. The atrocities happen, and she suffers. The shame, as I know that our children will blame us for today's mistakes, just as we blame the generations before us for the innocent blood they have spilt. The guilt as well. An age ago, on a school trip, a decrepit old lady pleaded to my tour group for help. She was in terrible health, her feet were almost bare, and she had a madness in her eyes. She wore the tattered rags of a t-shirt from a decade-old presidential campaign. We turned her away, believing, comfortable as we were, that we could do her no good. 

I raised the hopeful standard of the solarpunks. Vistas of cities and communities regenerating themselves through the resilience of community and the power of the Sun. A country, much like my own but stronger, wiser, and more compassionate, that embraces every refugee as its own, that draws strength from human ingenuity and renewable energy rather than from the dead plunder in the ground. The dream that such a future is possible, though its architects must give up everything and never cross into that promised land.

I drew upon Immanuel Kant's categorical imperatives. I found good advice within the traditional virtues. I reflected upon the righteous, selfless, and disinterested action espoused by the Bhagavad Gita. I admired the ardent faith and self-surrendering ethic of the Muslims, and wondered if I could ever find certainty as they do. I respected the universalizing spirit of the Sikhs and their Gurus. And from the Buddhists, I was reminded always that suffering is very real.

The multitude of worldviews and voices, quarrelsome and contentious without rest, had for once, converged as a chorus upon a single point - that is, that the suffering are entitled to the wealth that I currently possess.

I set aside an emergency fund from my own salary. For two working years, I gave everything above that to effective humanitarian aid, environmental causes, and community organizations. In that time, I started playing the violin again, exercising, writing, and working on personal projects. I found friends and mentors who would influence my life for the better.

I did not care one bit about my long-term financial security or retirement - I was a whirlwind, earning aggressively and giving fiercely, recognizing that my inherent capabilities were growing dramatically. ### The Grain of Wheat

*"Water the earth with your tears, and love those tears."*

\- Father Zossima, in *The Brothers Karamazov*

I could appreciate Dostoevsky back in high school. The characters behaved erratically, ranted feverishly, and were obviously in an unhealthy state of mind. I remembered being bewildered at Raskolnikov's delirium after he had committed his terrible crime, and wondered why he was unable to 'lock in' and complete the rest of his plan. Sonia's timid presence, rather than demonstrating the strength of her spirituality against despair, evoked in me a new determination to search for a *material* resolution to her conditions.

The one part of Dostoevsky's corpus that stuck with me throughout university was The Grand Inquisitor, and its enigmatic ending. I tried on many occasions to make sense of it, but I always left empty-handed.

It was 6 years later that I picked up Dostoevsky again, after I had graduated from university and started working. A major impetus was a discussion I had with a childhood friend, our class valedictorian, about how each of us should conduct our careers in an increasingly unstable and difficult global situation. The question that we both converged upon was this: 

*How must one act in a world without hope, without a future?*

Reading _Notes from the Underground_, I experienced the same despair and self-hatred that had haunted me after I had renounced my original convictions. 

And re-reading _The Brothers Karamazov_ was like being pierced in the heart. The characters, once uncoordinated and confusingly-written, were now vivid, symphathetic, agonizingly human. One by one, I became the characters, and through their dialogues and monologues, drew the disparate parts of my personality into conversation.

I became Alyosha and his struggle to maintain faith in God and to be a good person, in a world without direction. I became Dmitri, tortured by both his inexorable will to live and his incessant pursuit of oblivion. I became Ivan, crying out to heaven over the deaths of innocent children until cold rationality tells him that crying is useless. I became Smerdyakov too, driven to horrifying conclusions by ideas he had not understood completely.

And I was profoundly moved by the testament of Father Zossima. That our world is sustained by relationships with many other mysterious worlds, and that by turning away from this ecology we risk losing ourselves. That hell is not a physical place, but rather the torment of being unable to love. That through our ecological connection with everyone else, we become responsible for the sins of the world entire, and therefore we must be strong enough to forgive and to bear the consequences of the entire world.

The Grand Inquisitor, in his piercing criticism of this strand of Christ-like morality, maintains that Man will never be strong enough to endure this terrible burden, nor can he be loving and forgiving enough to create this *ecology of freedom*. All Man is is livestock, to be given feed instead of hope, to be shown certainty instead of faith, and to be taught submission instead of love, from the day of his birth to the moment of his slaughter.

The parable of the onion illustrates this cruel dichotomy of Man, of being able to envision the journey towards God while being incapable of undertaking it: 

---

A wicked woman dies and is tormented in Hell. Her guardian angel, residing in Heaven, desperately tries to rescue her by appealing to the Creator.

God says: *"What good deeds have this woman done on Earth?"*

*"Not many. But once, despite her poverty, she gave an onion to a destitute peasant."*

And God replies: *"Then take hold of the very same onion, and extend your arm over Hell. Let her grasp hold of the onion, and lift her out of Hell, so long as the onion does not break."*

The angel holds out the onion, and the woman takes hold of it. Inch by inch, the angel lifts her out of Hell.

Alas, the other souls in Hell see her being lifted up to Heaven. They clutch on to her knees and ankles, begging to be lifted out of the fire with her. The onion holds its shape.

The woman kicks downwards and yells: *"Get away from me! This is my onion!".*

The onion shatters. The woman remains in Hell. The angel covers her face in grief and retreats.

---

Encountering the parable of the onion, my thoughts return to the question of whether there is such a man who would willingly descend to Hell to take the place of his tormentor instead of enjoying eternal bliss, just as Jesus Christ was said to have done. And if such a man were to endure eternal torment willingly, for each and every one of his neighbors, he would be no less than greatest of all men. All the angels and saints and seekers would follow him to the ends of this world and into Hell itself.

If an angel were to offer an onion to such people, they would ensure that everyone else were lifted out first. If there was only one pre-ordained 'ticket' out of Hell for every five souls, by virtue of creed or confession, not one would accept it, as each is tormented by the suffering of their neighbour more than the torment itself.

And for whom do the fires of Hell still burn hot, if those that remain within it do so in a spirit of love, solidarity, and self-sacrifice? If Hell is a medieval torture-chamber created by God to punish heretics for eternity, nomatter how much they love their neighbors and God, then it is an admission that Hell is more powerful than love. But, if Hell is perpetuated by the collective rejection of fraternal and spiritual love, then by willingly choosing both love and Hell, Hell must be destroyed.

Where then, would such a man, such a community, be found to transform souls and to overcome Hell? How would they act within our world? And how does one become like them, pitiful desperate livestock as we may be?

Beyond the superficial duties of allocating assets for humanitarianism, or performing the noble work of mitigating climate change, a question about the human soul remains to be answered.

Years ago, Hayao Miyazaki's film, _Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind_, helped me find the courage I needed to complete my degree and to persist in working in climate change mitigation. Now, I turned to his magnum opus, the eponymous seven-book long epic underlying the film.

In Nausicaa, a young princess forced into an ecological catastrophe and an apocalyptic conflict to protect her people, I found a character taking responsibility for the sins of an entire world. From ecosystem collapse to refugee crises to genocide, she bears every transgression of our globe-spanning civilization, and she does so willingly, out of love, for every human and non-human person she encounters.

And in Nausicaa, I found someone who I would follow to the ends of the earth and into hell itself. The books themselves are a terrifying exploration of suffering and hell. As the reader loves the princess for her compassionate and self-sacrifical nature, one draw closer to her. When she can lift someone out of the incessant misery of her world, one experiences love towards them. When they succumb and die, Nausicaa's guilt at having been responsible and having failed becomes one's guilt as well. And when the world cries out in agony, when Nausicaa is overwhelmed and on the verge of collapse, one feels as though they are being crucified alongside her, as though they too are drawing towards themselves the sin of the entire human race.

One is left wondering if hell is to have never encountered Nausicaa, or to have loved her.

From Ivan's hatred of the incessant malice present in God's world, _The Brothers Karamazov_ builds a strong case against faith in God, and from the depraved nature of Man, a strong case against Man's capacity to follow a Messiah towards salvation. However, Dostoevsky then demonstrates the inadequacy of such a worldview in addressing the very problems it despises. Instead, one could love. A humble, selfless love that shoulders burdens and engenders self-sacrifice, a humanitarian love towards the Dmitris of our world, a love that sees past heaven and hell and recognizes the ecology of all things. And from that form of love, one regains themselves and their faith in God.

_Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind_ goes the other way. It starts by introducing the messianic figure of Nausicaa. Through Nausicaa's selfless, unconditional love towards all those she encounters, she transforms the nature of the conflict her world is embroiled in, awakening in friend and foe alike to the better angels of their nature and the possibility of salvation. Alas, the world has seen messiahs before, and has crushed them. Bit by bit, Nausicaa is confronted with the reality that she could not save everyone. That despite all her efforts to the contrary, she would be the cause of many deaths. That her love could cause her to kill and her hope would make her deceive. What is finally deconstructed isn't only the messiah embodied by Nausicaa, but the very promise of salvation in a fallen world.

What is left, then?

*"Except a corn of wheat fall into the ground and die, it abideth alone: but if it die, it bringeth forth much fruit" - The Brothers Karamazov*

*"For the sake of a single sprout, countless forest spores rain down again and again, dying a useless death" - Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind*

That we must live. When we devote our life and death to high ideals, it is likely that nothing will come of it, but for the sake of even one other person, we must continue. And for one person to receive the gifts of love, faith, and hope in their life, many other persons around the world, human and non-human, subsist in a truncated and deficient way. If someone constructs their denunciation of God's world from the suffering inflicted on those persons, they must either seek to eradicate the same suffering to remain consistent, even if it means rejecting the world entire, or to accept the absence of any values whatsover. However, if one creates their affirmation of life by the presence of these ideals, one is forced to live by them, and if necessary, to die for them.

And to know the price of the ideals that we embody is borne by an entire world of suffering, we must realize that each of us are responsible for the sins of an entire world. To maintain an affirmation of life from these ideals, one must find it within themselves to make amends for everyone. And for others to maintain an affirmation of life from these ideals, one must find it within themselves to forgive everyone. From this principle stems, like a natural ecology that lives within even the smallest leaf and the tiniest insect, an ecology of love that reaches from the depths of hell to the presence of God.

Therefore, one cannot hold both an affirmation of life from the presence of love, and a denunciation of God's world from a position of love, at the same time. 

God is alive in the beauty and the suffering of life's conversation with life. I choose to affirm life.

## Part 3: Affirmation towards Displacement

### Hypocrisy and Genocide

*Can we be good people? Or have we just been pretending to be good people, all this while?"*

At the 2023 Fridays for the Future climate march, I reflected on the results of this worldview, and found that it was good. I was doing good work that I was proud of, working on personal projects, improving my technical skills, practising the violin, and exercising. Each month, I gave almost all of my disposable income for humanitarian and environmental needs, and I knew with certainty that many lives were being saved and improved because of this. Horrible crimes were being perpetrated around the world, but so long as I utterly commit to their resolution, I could be content.

There was nothing left for me to do, therefore, nothing to feel anguished about.

One month later, Palestinian militants attempted to reclaim their country by force, and the Israeli military responded by attempting genocide.

Journalists were being murdered everywhere. But, the footage that did come out were a vista of hell. Entire neighborhoods being ground to dust. Skeletal children sifting flour from the dirt. Bloodied stumps of limbs beyond counting, and rows of white shrouds beyond number. Furthermore, this was not the brutish macheting of unsophisticated third world warlords, but rather a genocide perpetrated with the  knowledge, consent, and capability of the most developed, most advanced, and most moralizing countries of the world.

The Western moral canon has given birth to democracy, the rule of law, virtue ethics, universalizable principles, and consequentialism. It has laid the foundations of modern humanitarianism and environmentalism. International visitors such as myself see great value in learning about these ideas to better our own countries. Even the horrible crimes of these powerful countries in colonialism, genocide, and environmental destruction were worth learning about to avoid repeating. 

To see the most *moral* countries on the planet once again walking back on their principles when it mattered most, as if nothing of consequence had been learnt in the preceding hundred years, filled me with a rage and disgust I had not felt since the time I renounced my God, many years ago. I spent week after week protesting the culpable institutions and the government, with hundreds of thousands of others, to no avail.

At one such demonstration, there was a cry of "From Turtle Island to Palestine" that gave us all pause. At that moment, we all remembered that the very ground we stand on, the land we had immigrated to find hope and to affirm life, had been stolen from the Musqueam and Coast Salish First Nations, the same way that settlers now dispossess Palestinians of their homes and land. As before, if we felt rage and disgust at the Israelis, we must also be enraged and disgusted with ourselves.

My company laid me off, when I had most expected a promotion for my work. Unwanted gears as I was at the time, tumbling away from the great machinery that I helped build and that had built my career, I gained clarity about the grand revolutions of the industry I had put my life into. Magnitudes of power, channels of force, and threads of influence, crisscrossing worlds seen and unseen. I saw the tinyness and insignificance of my two years' of hard work, as much as I liked to believe that my sacrifices would be worth it, justifiable, meaningful.

Several months of savings, with some extra from tax returns and severance pay - this was what I had to find a new job in the same field. Every month, I saw my savings wear thinner and thinner, and every month, I debated cutting off my humanitarian contributions to double my own time. But each time, I remembered that though my situation was difficult, there are those in this world who live on the brink.

In a moment of personal crisis, I wondered if I had made a mistake coming here to study and to work, and that by extension I had misused the resources entrusted to me across my lifetime. If the good done by me being here is less than the public wealth increased by my parents simply spending the cost of my education on vacations and leisure, then I should not be here.

Nervously, I did the economic calculation, tabulating the entire cost of my education against the opportunities to do good afforded to me by my education. I then compared the number of livelihoods that would have been supported in Indonesia by the cost of my education reduced by the number of livelihoods supported in Canada by my tuition fees, to the number of livelihoods made possible by the humanitarian aid I have given. I then applied two principles: (1) A human life anywhere is worth exactly the same, and (2) A human livelihood made possible anywhere is worth *almost* exactly the same, because basic human needs can be met very inexpensively anywhere, but higher needs require more significant investment.

The results were startling. 23 person-years of the average Indonesian livelihood, pouring upwards into my parent's investments, had been invested into my education. Between 16 - 130 person-years of livelihood have been made possible by my humanitarian contributions. It is therefore, extremely likely that my journey to this point had not been a mistake.

There was a sense of elationthe darkness of my own humanity. at first, but once again, the same emptiness. I had just tried to prove my own goodness and justify my privileges with economics. If someone in a difficult situation were to perform the same calculation, even if they were a heroic revolutionary or saint dependent on the financing of others, they would be trapped in a societal debt so deep as to appear as no recourse were possible.

The important question isn't 'can you justify your privileges and sense of goodness' - when one and one's world is complicit in genocide, there can be no justifications of privilege, nor proof of beneficience. 

The more important question is: 'who are you, when you have no privilege and have no proof of your own goodness'? Are all my deeds and moral sense founded on just being healthy and well-off in the most affluent and stable part of the world, or do they stand on the fundamental principles I claim to uphold?

I met someone, at a protest to protect the land rights of indigenous communities. A fellow Chinese Indonesian, a talented artist, sophisticated yet grounded, brilliant yet humble. She has a startling intuition of morality and of the form of right action, and I saw her employ her artistic and organizational skill to raise funds for the causes she values the most. The subtle strength of her intellect and the persistence of her compassion in the face of despair shines through her art and into our conversations. When we find the time to sit down and speak at length, hours go by in instants. In opposition to the dull cliches which have suppressed the moral, societal, and political development of my entire ethnic community, she was hope and dynamism and change.

I asked if we could be together. It was not to be. We are too similar. We are too different.

I got a new job across the country. Packing up my belongings and saying goodbye, I realized just how much community and friendship I was leaving behind. Watching the sun set and rise again for the last time on the dark waters and forested mountains and glittering skyscrapers of Vancouver, I reflected on the beauty of this part of the world, and how lucky I am to have lived seven years of my life here. And moving across the country by train, across the snowcapped mountain ranges, prairie, forests and lakes, and conversing with the diverse groups of people I met along the way, I am once again reminded of the largness of the world, beyond the confines of my upbringing.

### Displacement

_"Who are you to affirm life? Can you affirm yourself?"_

Being unemployed and having to uproot myself across the country pushed my financial situation to an uncomfortable position. I had to question my assumptions of abundance, that there will always be more to become, more to do, more to earn, and more to give. I realized that as my work became ever more specialized and valuable, I had to set aside ever-larger buffers for rainy days. Reluctantly, I halved my humanitarian contributions to rebuild my rainy day fund, knowing full well that the humanitarian situation remained critical. Not 'me or them', I told myself, but 'me for them'. 

I arrived in Guelph, a small city in southern Ontario. I deliberately chose humbler accomodations, and I ended up downgrading from a private studio to a 3-bedroom apartment with 4 other people. What I gained from this, however, wasn't just savings. It was perspective.

Two of the tenants were international students at a local college, who were simultaneously working multiple service-sector jobs to cover their living expenses, their tuition, and their remittances to their families. The other two had advanced degrees from their home countries, yet were working semi-skilled assembly-line jobs in the hopes that they could resume their career path. They had next to nothing, but from the day I walked into their apartment, they offered me what they had. Flatbread and curry, friendship and respect.

One of them, a small Gujarati man, confided in me of his situation - his father, the breadwinner of his family, had died during the COVID-19 pandemic, leaving behind an aging mother and two sisters. Having obtained an engineering degree in India, he moved abroad to obtain an associate degree, hoping to earn enough working part-time jobs to support his family. The diploma mill he attended took the lion's share of his salary, while yielding no useful skills in return. His Canadian employers paid him in cash - that is, pitifully and unreliably.

So this is the person accused of destroying the Canadian economy and its future. A South Asian of talents comparable to my own, possessing both resilience from a life of hardship, and erudition from an educated upbringing. A man with whom I could spend hours in discussion about worldviews and principles, culture and history, relationships and family. Ambitious and opportunistic, yes, but no more than anyone building a life in a new country. For my friend to be the target of widespread vitriol, not the consultants who peddled immigration fraud or the diploma mills who sold false hope, not the franchisees who undercut wages to subsistence, and not the government who turned a blind eye to such exploitation, opened my eyes to another fundamental injustice built into Canadian society.

Our presence in this country by education and white collar work is no more righteous and valuable than their presence by sweat and tears. Yes, they exploited a technicality in the Canadian immigration system to start a new life here, but we exploited 23 years of someone else's livelihood for the express purpose of perpetuating privilege.

Just as the immigration system lets individuals climb up in standing towards acceptance and residence, it is also a means by which prospective immigrants kick each other down the ladder. The graduates of prestigious research universities blaming the graduates of diploma mills for their own inability to attain residence. Within diploma mills, ethnic group blaming ethnic group for boarding the gravy train that had served their predecessors so well. And finally, bitter rage and hatred towards refugees and asylum seekers, who having crossed oceans of thirst and chasms of hunger for their lives, desperately need residence more than any of us.

To be a migrant again, and to understand the uncertainty of my position in this country, gave me a newfound 'commitment to place'. That even if one must inevitably uproot themselves again, one still has a responsibility to the places they encounter and to the people they meet. I began volunteering at a local charity and found great joy in helping the locals in their projects and crafts. I made friends who I might never see again. I planted a small garden and watched the flowers bloom and attract butterflies before they wilted and fell.

I rebuilt what I had neglected in the chaotic upheaval of my migration. I completed several personal projects, excelled at my workplace, exceeded my personal running record, reached a new level of proficiency at the violin, got better at Chinese and French, and even hiked the 70 kilometres from Guelph to Toronto. I connected with friends new and old. When my savings sat comfortably again, I started increasing my charitable donations again, this time supporting my local community. I set aside a fund from my savings to support my parents' retirement. In measurable terms, this was turning out to be my best year yet.

### Despair

*Look at your feet. Among the dead there are those you killed yourself. How dare you feign innocence? Try all you like to remain an undefiled child. But it won't do you any good... You are nothing more than just another foolish, filthy human being. A bloody woman of a cursed people. You should be writhing there along with the dead!! Soon enough you will come to recognize my virtue.*

\- The Voice of Nothingness to Nausicaa, *Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind*

And yet, I could not shake the idea that I was, at my core, a hollow being, a shell of a person. Somewhere along the way, I had abandoned the traditional milestones of adulthood and replaced them with an overriding directive: _Mitigate climate change, improve your community, and take care of your parents. Or die trying._ My hobbies and interests, even my irrational cravings, were all just there to shore up my sanity, instead of being inherently valuable elements of my personality. My interactions with others were just faces I wear. Aside from several core principles and memories, I wondered if I had any personality at all.

_Ubuntu_. The idea that a person is a person through other people. Not through the bestowal of a higher power, not through an act of spontaneous self-affirmation, but through ecology itself. If there is any self to me at all, it only exists as part of a larger system. I've let my interactions define me, instead of the other way around.

If the world is so vast, magnificent, and beautiful, and if I have had the opportunity to build meaningful relationships across many parts of the world, then why am I still so small, incomplete, and ignoble?

I've been waiting, for years now, for a new moment of enlightenment. Water overflowing their reservoirs, streams colliding and joining with force, a great river surging forwards towards perfection. I would drown in this flow, be consumed by it, and give life to a hundred thousand trees before reaching the ocean. But instead, from a muddy, stagnant, pool, my own reflection stares back.

And so I come to understand again despair. The constant that binds my past and present together, as well as my future. Nomatter how eloquently I can articulate my convictions, nomatter how much I convince myself that a better person has emerged out of the old, in truth I have not been able to escape my flaws. Despite the light I have seen, I cannot escape the darkness of my own humanity.

No one can. From conflict to conflict, atrocity to atrocity, genocide to genocide - we have not learnt anything. We remain cursed to repeat our mistakes over and over again. Words like _gratitude_ and _affirmation_ turn to ash and dust in my mouth as I recall the hundreds of thousands dead.

Each of us, we are not only responsible for the sins of the world, in the sense that we must forgive and make amends for the whole world. We are _complicit_ in them, such that every one of our blessings and crimes are borne out of a sea of spilled blood and shattered bodies. Every word of gratitude, every moment of affirmation, any earthly heaven we humans can contrive on our own - all emerge from the darkness, and all are cursed to be displaced back into the darkness.

### Flowers Blooming in the Church

*Even if our bodies cannot tolerate that purity... even if the moment we are exposed to it, we spew blood from our lungs... just as the birds migrate across the land, we shall live, and live again.*

\- Nausicaa, in *Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind*Who do you affirm

*I see the sun, and if I don't see the sun, I know it's there. And there's a whole life in that, in knowing that the sun is there.*

\- Dmitri Karamazov, in *The Brothers Karamazov*

We are doomed to fall - this was always how our story was going to end. And yet, we continue to tell stories of other possibilities. One of a universal law of justice that apportions reward and punishment across lifetimes, or of a system for all beings to extinguish the vicious cycle of consequence we are all trapped within. Or a story that all good and evil are but part of the will of God, or that once, a supreme act of self-sacrifice liberated all from the burden of sin. 

We continue to tell each other stories, despite them being exercises in failure, because they bring us to confront, and to ultimately come to an understanding with, the nature of other persons, and our own natures.

What is faith, but to have such a connection with a story, that one would think and act as though it were true? 

What is hope, but to be so deeply invested in a story that one would do anything to make it true?

And what is love, but to live and die so that the truth of a story will be heard? 

The stories we tell ourselves, age after age, even the ones arising out of chaos and darkness, tell us that these transcendent qualities arise again and again within us, though we are unworthy of them.

Tapestries of wild flowers, blossoming from a barren ruins into a tiny ecology, climbing towards the light, before finally falling away so that a new generation can rise again. How wonderful would it be, if this small ecology is but a shattered reflection of a greater ecology, one that does not fade or diminish, but rather perpetuates itself through all seasons, contains itself in its infinite complexity, one and all together! 

However, isn't it enough of a miracle that barrenness can give way to life, even for an instant?

I no longer believe that the purpose of Christianity can be the individual salvation of our souls. For what we are all complicit in, we are all going to hell, if we aren't there already. We can yearn for, but we certainly cannot *expect*, something that we obviously do not deserve.

What I believe our purpose is is to evoke in each other faith out of nihilism, hope out of despair, and love out of apathy. The resilience to rise again after overwhelming death and loss, and the grace to fall and to die to allow another to begin anew. 

And if this path only leads to destruction, then so be it. If Christianity meant anything at all, then this has always been worth going to hell for.

### Failing Upwards

_" I can hear a voice in my heart, all the time. Go forward, it tells me. So all I think of now is of going forward, as far as I am able."_

\- Nausicaa, *Nausicaa of the Valley of the Wind*

Today, I return home to Indonesia, after spending one third of my life abroad. I had little choice in this matter; my 3-year work permit has ended, and despite my best efforts, I was unable to secure residency.

In this, I have failed, yet again. I know that in the months to come, I will feel an intense nostalgia for the snowcapped mountains and forested valleys of British Columbia, the humble beauty of Guelph, the golden fields of the Ontario countryside, and the vibrant cityscape of Toronto. I will regret not being able to earn as high of a salary and to give as much to humanitarian aid. I will miss my friends, who will now live an ocean away, forever.

As of now, though, I feel a resolute calm. Barring a few points, I am content with what I have done with my opportunities here, for myself, my community, and internationally. I've not become the person 17-year old me would have wanted to become, 8 years ago, nor have I become the unambiguous success story that my parents wanted. Instead, I see the strange loop of displacement, gratitude, and affirmation arcing back onto itself, opening the way once more.

And though I fail again, this time I fail _upwards_.

Let me tell you a story about home. It is a place where the sun is bright and warm but the sky is always shrouded by smog. It is a place where the rain falls hard and long, but the waters are poisoned. It is a land that is green and rich above and below, but where millions struggle to survive. 

Let me tell you how such a land sent me across the world. Thousands of tonnes of minerals wrested from the earth and burnt in faraway factories. Decades of wealth accumulated from investments, from the labour of my family and country, and spent on tuition. For me to have seen what I have seen and done what I have done, immense sacrifices have been made by my parents, my country, the very earth itself. Indeed, the ones to whom I still owe a debt of gratitude are back home, waiting for me.

Let me explain what needs to be done to mitigate climate change in my country. Hundreds of gigawatts of both centralized and distributed solar power and energy storage to be brought online, an aging power grid to be modernized to support the operational complexity of 100% renewable energy. A thousand industries to be electrified and decarbonized. Millions of jobs to be created, tens of millions of people to be lifted out of poverty.

And finally, let me warn you how I will fail again, in my small contribution to this great work. I will do my job well, but be unable to effect the systematic progress I desire. I will produce innovative energy transition models but struggle to find audiences for them. I will try to change the system from within, as I have attempted before, but face entrenched resistance from all directions. I will struggle with language, religious, and cultural barriers. I will lose my nerve the first few times I try to do business and make terrible strategic decisions. I will lose friends and make enemies. 

Once again, it is time to see how far I will fall, and how high I can rise once more.

_"Look at yourself. You've grown stronger and wiser using your opportunities, but it isn't enough. It will never be enough for you, not until you believe you have saved the world. And so, failure is inevitable to you."_

_"Give yourself some space."_

_And try again."_
